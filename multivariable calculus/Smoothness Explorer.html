01 / Gradient → change of gradient

A gradient is a slope, in one dimension.

\(f(t)=\frac{L}{2}t^2,\qquad f'(t)=Lt\)
Functionx and its tangenty and its tangent

The vertical scale stays fixed as L changes, so increased curvature stays visible. Use sliders or drag near either point.

What am I seeing?L controls how rapidly the slope is allowed to change with position. Here the slope difference exactly equals L times the distance, for every pair of points. A large slope far from zero can still have a small rate of change.
02 / Lipschitz gradient → bounded curvature

Curvature can vary, while staying bounded.

\(f(t)=\log(\cosh t),\quad f'(t)=\tanh t,\quad f''(t)=\operatorname{sech}^2t\le1\)

Tangents change quickly near zero, slowly in the tails.

The shaded interval connects the two points to the curvature between them.

What am I seeing?The slope-change ratio is the average curvature over the shaded interval for this function. Move both points close to zero: the ratio approaches 1. Move them into a tail: it approaches 0. The smallest global L is 1, not just the ratio at the selected pair.
\(|f'(x)-f'(y)|\le L|x-y|\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |f''(t)|\le L\ \text{everywhere}\)

For twice-differentiable functions on an interval. In nonconvex examples curvature can be negative; the bound is on its absolute value.

03 / Gradient and curvature in space

One direction can be much steeper.

\(f(x_1,x_2)=\tfrac12(ax_1^2+bx_2^2),\quad\nabla f=(ax_1,bx_2),\quad L=\max(a,b)\)

Drag the surface to rotate. Height uses a fixed scale.

Click or drag to move the selected point. Arrows share scale 0.16; lengths represent gradient magnitudes.

What am I seeing?The blue gradient at x is perpendicular to the dashed level-curve tangent; the green negative gradient gives locally steepest descent among unit directions in Euclidean geometry. Orange shows the gradient at y. Narrower contours mean faster change of gradient in that direction. At the origin the gradient is zero, so there is no distinguished descent direction.
\(\nabla^2f=\mathrm{diag}(a,b),\qquad \|\nabla^2f\|_2=\max(a,b)=L\)

The largest eigenvalue determines the bound here because a,b are positive. For a general symmetric Hessian use the largest absolute eigenvalue.

04 / Bounded curvature → quadratic upper bound

Give the tangent room to bend.

Keep the true function fixed: f(t) = log(cosh t), whose global smoothness constant is 1. Vary the proposed bound M independently. M plays the role of L only when it is a valid bound.

\(Q_x(t)=f(x)+f'(x)(t-x)+\frac M2(t-x)^2\)
Actual functionDashed tangentQuadratic modelTrue function exceeds model
What am I seeing?The tangent assumes the gradient stays constant. L tells us how quickly the gradient is allowed to change, so the quadratic term compensates for the possible curvature. M ≥ 1 guarantees the upper bound for every anchor and every target. M < 1 has no such global guarantee, even if this particular anchor looks fine.
Why is the correction exactly quadratic?
\(f(y)-f(x)-f'(x)(y-x)=\int_0^1[f'(x+s(y-x))-f'(x)](y-x)\,ds\le\int_0^1 Ls|y-x|^2\,ds=\frac L2|y-x|^2.\)

The allowed slope error grows linearly with distance. Its accumulated area is a triangle: one half × distance × allowed slope change. The same argument along a line segment works in higher dimensions.

What am I seeing?At the model minimum, the quadratic correction to the slope exactly cancels the current slope. A larger L bends the model upward faster, bringing its minimum closer to x. The moving dot lands on the true function directly below the model minimum.
\(f(x^+)\le Q_x(x^+)=f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}|f'(x)|^2.\)
06 / Step size meets directional curvature

Careful steps. Zigzags. Escape.

Uses a and b from section 03. Click the contour to choose a starting point. Try the elongated-valley preset, then compare step sizes.

\(x_{k+1,i}=(1-\alpha\lambda_i)x_{k,i},\qquad 0<\alpha<2/L\ \Rightarrow\ \text{convergence}.\)
What am I seeing?A large L means high curvature in at least one direction. Near 2/L that coordinate flips sign at each step, making zigzags. At 2/L its magnitude stays constant. Beyond 2/L a nonzero component in the highest-curvature direction grows; a starting point exactly without that component can behave differently. The step 1/L is safe, but small curvature in another direction can still make convergence slow.
