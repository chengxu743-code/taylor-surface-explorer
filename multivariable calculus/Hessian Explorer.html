What am I looking at?

1. The Hessian measures bending

The gradient measures slope. The Hessian measures how that slope changes. Here, the gradient at the origin is zero, so curvature can classify the stationary point.

Pick a unit direction d = (cos θ, sin θ). Walking along (x,y) = td gives the slice f(td) = ½kt², where k = dᵀHd. Positive k bends up. Negative k bends down.

2. Eigenvalues test every direction

The eigenvalues are the smallest and largest directional curvatures. Their eigenvectors give the corresponding principal directions, shown as dashed lines in the ground plane.

All positive means a bowl; all negative means a cap. Mixed signs mean a saddle: nearby points go both up and down.

Try these five experiments
  1. Choose Bowl. Rotate θ from 0° to 180°. Every slice bends up.
  2. Choose Cap. Every slice now bends down.
  3. Choose Saddle. Compare θ = 0°, 45°, and 90°. The slice bends up, becomes flat, then bends down.
  4. Choose Hidden saddle. Both diagonal entries are positive. The x and y slices bend up, but θ = 135° bends down. Positive diagonal entries do not prove convexity.
  5. Choose Flat valley. At θ = 90°, the quadratic is exactly flat. Every point on x = 0 is a global minimum, so the minimum is not strict.
How does this connect to convexity?

For a twice continuously differentiable function on an open convex domain, convexity is equivalent to a positive semidefinite Hessian at every point. This means every directional curvature is nonnegative. Concavity uses nonpositive curvature instead.

Our quadratic has the same Hessian everywhere, so one calculation classifies its global convexity. A general function can change curvature from place to place. Checking one point is not enough.

For a convex differentiable function, a stationary point is a global minimum. For a concave one, it is a global maximum. Positive definite Hessians everywhere are sufficient, but not necessary, for strict convexity: x⁴ is strictly convex despite zero second derivative at zero.

Why a zero eigenvalue needs care

The quadratic model has no hidden higher-order terms, so its flat directions really are flat. General functions can behave differently: x⁴ + y⁴, −x⁴ − y⁴, and x⁴ − y⁴ all have the zero Hessian at the origin, yet have a strict minimum, strict maximum, and saddle respectively.

Why stationarity and boundaries matter

A positive definite Hessian does not mean the point you are standing on is a minimum. For example, x² + y² + x has Hessian 2I everywhere, but its minimum is at (−½,0), not the origin.

The usual stationary-point test concerns unconstrained interior points. On x ≥ 0, the function f(x) = x has a minimum at the boundary x = 0 even though its derivative is 1.

The calculation behind the picture

H = [[a,b],[b,c]]. Its eigenvalues are (a+c)/2 ± √(((a−c)/2)²+b²). Directional curvature is a cos²θ + 2b sinθ cosθ + c sin²θ.

The local second-order approximation of a general smooth function is f(p+Δ) ≈ f(p) + ∇f(p)ᵀΔ + ½ΔᵀH(p)Δ. This program displays the quadratic curvature term exactly, centered at a stationary origin.
